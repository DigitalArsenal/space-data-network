Encrypted conjunction assessment

Private screening

Two operators learn whether their satellites will come too close, without either seeing the other’s orbit. Private screening is specified and measured; it is not yet available on the network.

The problem

Why Operators Keep Their Best Data Private

The most precise orbits and planned maneuvers are what keeps satellites apart, and what operators can least afford to publish.

National security

Classified orbits reveal capabilities, coverage gaps and mission intent to adversaries.

Commercial value

Constellation geometry, station-keeping strategy and coverage patterns are trade secrets worth billions.

Maneuver plans

A planned burn tells competitors and adversaries that something is about to change, and when.

Liability

Sharing creates obligations: an operator that shares ephemeris and then misses a conjunction may carry more liability than one that shared nothing.

How it works

Screening Without Sharing

Homomorphic encryption lets a computer do arithmetic on locked numbers. It adds and multiplies them without unlocking them, and the answer stays locked.

Your key stays with you

You lock your trajectory with your own key before it leaves your systems. Nobody else can unlock it.

The other side computes

The other operator works out, on the locked numbers, how close its own satellite comes at each moment. It never sees your orbit, and its own never leaves its systems.

Only you open the answer

You unlock one answer for each moment: too close, or not. No third party takes part, and each operator runs it to protect its own satellite.

The arithmetic is |a − b|² = |a|² − 2a·b + |b|², on positions locked with Microsoft SEAL (BFV, 128-bit security), 8,192 moments to each locked block. A separate assessor would add no privacy: whoever can unlock the answer could also unlock the positions it was sent.

What it reveals

What Each Side Learns

The answer is meant to say when the other satellite comes too close, and nothing more.

Only the close moments

You learn the moments when the other satellite is within the safety distance. Not where it is, and not how far away it is the rest of the time.

One yes or no per moment

Hiding the distance behind a random multiplier is not enough: in our tests the distance could be recovered within a factor of two at 87% of moments. The answer is therefore a single yes or no per moment, produced by a two-party comparison.

Real close approaches show

In three days, 2,532 objects came within 5 km of a Starlink satellite, half of them three or more times. Any safe system reports those, and enough of them reveal an orbit to the neighbor that sees them.

Defenses

Protection Against Orbit Guessing

Could someone submit invented orbits to find a hidden satellite? Without defenses, yes: about 1,200 questions find one whose altitude is roughly known. In the design, each satellite’s owner enforces these defenses on its own node.

Enforced by the owner

Every answer about your satellite comes from your node, computed with your orbit. Others can run any software they like, modified or not; they cannot make your node answer more, skip a check or drop the noise. Locked data is useless without its owner’s key.

Questions must match a real satellite

Each question has to stay close to the public track of a satellite the asker actually operates, with room for a declared maneuver. Your node checks this on the locked values, learns only pass or fail, and refuses invented orbits before answering.

Opened afterwards

Each time window is locked with a fresh key, and your node keeps exactly what it answered. Once the window has passed, the asker hands over that key, and your node opens what it was sent and checks every moment against independent tracking and orbital physics. A withheld key or a failed check forfeits the asker’s stake and ends its screening.

Fixed rules, limited questions

Positions come at fixed times set by the protocol, and every moment of a window is checked. Each pair of satellites gets one signed answer per window, and no asker may ask about more satellites per window than it has registered. That caps what anyone could learn before a failed check catches them.

Blurred distances

Your node adds random noise (Laplace noise) to each distance before the yes-or-no test, so no answer says exactly how close. The test threshold is raised to match, so the noise can add false alarms but never hides a real one, and the same question always gets the same noise.

Partners for hidden satellites

A satellite missing from the public catalog cannot pass a public-track check. Its owner screens only with counterparties it agrees to work with, and they with it.

Both directions work the same way: when two operators screen each other, each protects its own orbit with its own node. Open source does not weaken this; what protects an orbit is its owner’s key and its owner’s node, not secrecy of the code. What remains is what any safe system reveals: real close approaches between real satellites, and, for an asker willing to lose its identity, at most one time window of questions near its own satellites.

Decoys

Hiding Among Look-Alikes

The owner of a hidden satellite can submit many candidate orbits, only one of them real. Only its key says which answers matter.

One real among many

Someone guessing orbits finds mostly decoys. An owner that always sends many candidates also hides whether it has a hidden satellite at all.

Decoys must look real

Decoys must persist from day to day, move like real satellites, and cannot fool anyone who tracks the satellite directly.

Measured, not yet ready

We tested five ways of making decoys on 3,000 real satellites. The best hid a real orbit among about 4 of 100 candidates, not 100. Decoys add a layer behind the checks above; they do not replace them.
